Interactive Layout Design and Evaluation

Design a layout and evaluate it two ways: against the qualitative proximity relationships it was meant to honour, and against the travel its flows actually generate. Drag the centers and every score follows on the spot. Loaded with the Layout 0 case throughout.

Relationship model: Evans, G.W., M.R. Wilhelm & W. Karwowski (1987), A layout design heuristic employing the theory of fuzzy sets, International Journal of Production Research, v25 no10, 1431–1450. Case and encoding: Montreuil, B. (2008), Facilities Location and Layout Design, ch. 5.

Centers

The set of centers is declared here, and the other two sections read it. A center exists because it is listed, not because some relationship mentions it — a monument, a remnant machine or a structural column belongs to no relationship at all, yet it occupies space and traffic has to go round it. Give each one a short name and say what it is; place it by dragging, or by typing its position below. Drag a center to move it, drag the square at its lower-right corner to resize, and drag a triangle to slide that station along its walls. A hollow triangle pointing inward is an input station, a solid one pointing outward an output, and a kite one that serves as both. Every center starts with an input and an output; add as many more as it needs — material in, waste out, people in and out — each with its own id, function and place on a wall. Flows still travel between a center’s first input and first output; routing them through a named station is the next step. Traced from the course drawing — the block positions reproduce the centroid distances the course reports for B–D, B–C and C–D. The aisle check below measures the space those blocks leave between them.

Aisle check

The clear space left between two centers that face each other, with nothing standing in between — which is where an aisle would have to go. A gap narrower than a single lane cannot carry traffic at all, however much flow wants to cross it, so those are called out first. The lane width used here is the one set on the Design skeleton tab, as is the narrower walking lane for aisles that only people use.

Satisfaction curves

One curve per desired proximity. Read a relationship's desired proximity from the table, take its measured distance along the bottom, and the curve gives the satisfaction that multiplies its weight. A ramp runs between the distance where satisfaction is 1 and the distance where it is 0; bend curves it — 1 is a straight line, above 1 drops off faster at first, below 1 holds on longer. A bell peaks at its ideal distance and falls away on both sides, reaching half satisfaction one half-width out; it is the only form that can say “not too far, not too near”, where closer is not always better. The axis runs to 30 m — raise it for a larger facility.

Flow estimation matrix

Show empty trips Hide stations with no flow

Enter the loaded trips per period from each center to every other — these come from the process and organization design, or from flow tracking. A loaded trip runs from the source center's output station to the destination's input station. A figure you type turns green, because it is the only thing on this page that is not computed: the empty trips that return each vehicle to its next pickup, and every margin total, all follow from it. The diagonal takes no input, because a center never ships a loaded trip to itself.

Once centers carry several stations the matrix grows faster than it stays readable, so the flows can be typed as a list instead — one line per flow that actually runs, naming the station it leaves and the station it arrives at. The matrix below then depicts what the list says; it is the same figures either way.

Shaded italics are empty trips, running the other way round — from the source center's input station to the destination's output station, because a vehicle that has just unloaded is standing at the input dock. They are placed so that every center balances, arrivals equalling departures, at the least total empty travel. The diagonal carries the cheapest move of all: unload and reload within the same center.

Flow graph / heatmap

The same picture the course builds up a layer at a time: the desired proximities the layout was designed around, and the traffic that actually runs over them. The three layers are independent — turn on whichever combination you want to look at. Each route follows the same aisle path its distance was measured along, so nothing here crosses a block — a vehicle goes round, exactly as it would on the floor. Arrow width is proportional to trips per period. The heatmap adds the traffic up aisle by aisle instead, which is where congestion shows.

The subset band narrows the picture to the links that carry the most, ranked within whatever is turned on: flows by trips, relationships by importance weight. [90–100]% is the heaviest tenth, [20–50]% hides the quietest fifth and everything above the median. Links of equal weight rank together and are kept or dropped together, so asking for the most important always returns the whole most-important class. Flows can be read station by station or gathered to the centers they run between.

Distance matrix

Show every pair

How far each of those trips actually travels. Nothing here is typed in — every figure is measured on the layout above, so moving a center or sliding a station changes this table at once. A cell carries a figure only where the matrix above runs trips; tick Show every pair to measure the whole grid.

The two entries in a cell are measured between different points. A loaded trip leaves the source center's output station and ends at the destination's input station. An empty trip does the reverse — it starts where the vehicle just unloaded, the source's input station, and ends where it picks up next, the destination's output station. On the diagonal the vehicle crosses its own center from input dock to output dock, so that leg is measured straight through the block rather than around it.

Travel evaluation

Trips times distance, cell by cell — the metres a vehicle covers per period on each move. This is the number a layout is judged on: the two matrices above are its two halves, and everything a redesign is trying to cut shows up here. The margins split loaded from empty, because a metre driven empty earns nothing.

The average columns are metres per trip, not metres per period: total travel divided by the trips that made it. A center with a high average is one whose work arrives from far away, which is the case for moving it — high traffic at a short average is already well placed.

Design skeleton

Flows Relationships I/O stations

Before a layout has shapes it has a topology: what sits next to what, and which pairs have to stay apart. The skeleton is that topology, laid out from whichever links the subset band in the flow section has left standing — so narrow the band to the heaviest tenth, draw a skeleton on it, then widen the band and refine. Every link pulls its two centers toward the separation it wants: a flow simply wants to be short, while a proximity relationship wants the distance at which it is satisfied, which for Far means pushing apart rather than together.

Accepted skeletons

Block layout from the skeleton

The skeleton says where things stand relative to one another; a block layout gives them their real size inside the real building. Each node swells into a rectangle of the area its center needs, in the shape it already has, at gross size — larger than the net figure, to leave the room the aisles will take. Rectangles that end up on top of one another are pushed apart along whichever axis they overlap least, which is the move that disturbs the skeleton’s arrangement least. Accepting a layout puts it on the Layout page, where it is scored and edited like any other.

These scores are taken without aisles, because at this stage there are none: the space the gross blocks hold is what the aisles will be cut from at the next stage. Every alternative is measured the same way — rectilinear by default — which is what makes them comparable with each other. Choosing around the blocks shows what the layout would cost if no aisle were ever cut, and Blocked trips counts the traffic that would then have nowhere to go. Empty trips are re-solved for every alternative, because moving the centers changes which return legs are cheapest.

Net layout and aisle set

Move centers to fit the aisles Keep a way out of the building
Show building centers I/O stations aisles travel network

The centers come back from their gross size to the size they actually need, and the space that opens is where the aisles go. The aisle set is not guessed at: every loaded and every empty trip is routed through the freed space, and the runs traffic actually uses become the segments. A segment gets as many lanes as its traffic needs — a single two-way lane while it stays quiet, a lane each way once it does not — and where two segments cross they make an intersection as wide as the lanes meeting there. Empty trips count throughout, because a vehicle returning for its next load occupies a lane exactly as a loaded one does.

Left alone, the aisles take the room the layout already leaves them. Move centers to fit the aisles instead gives the lanes the width their traffic asks for and pushes the centers aside to make it — north, east, south or west, as the specification puts it. On a tight building that costs proximity score and can press a center against the wall, so both are reported: how far the centers were shifted, how much was pushed off the plan, and how much of the width the traffic asked for the floor could actually give.
