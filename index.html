Interactive Layout Design and Evaluation

Design a layout and evaluate it two ways: against the qualitative proximity relationships it was meant to honour, and against the travel its flows actually generate. Drag the centers and every score follows on the spot. Loaded with the Layout 0 case throughout.

Relationship model: Evans, G.W., M.R. Wilhelm & W. Karwowski (1987), A layout design heuristic employing the theory of fuzzy sets, International Journal of Production Research, v25 no10, 1431–1450. Case and encoding: Montreuil, B. (2008), Facilities Location and Layout Design, ch. 5.

Centers

The set of centers is declared here, and the other two sections read it. A center exists because it is listed, not because some relationship mentions it — a monument, a remnant machine or a structural column belongs to no relationship at all, yet it occupies space and traffic has to go round it. Give each one a short name and say what it is; place it by dragging, or by typing its position below. Drag a center to move it, drag the square at its lower-right corner to resize, and drag a triangle to slide that station along its walls. A hollow triangle pointing inward is an input station, a solid one pointing outward an output, and a kite one that serves as both. Every center starts with an input and an output; add as many more as it needs — material in, waste out, people in and out — each with its own id, function and place on a wall. Flows still travel between a center’s first input and first output; routing them through a named station is the next step. Traced from the course drawing — the block positions reproduce the centroid distances the course reports for B–D, B–C and C–D.

Satisfaction curves

One curve per desired proximity. Read a relationship's desired proximity from the table, take its measured distance along the bottom, and the curve gives the satisfaction that multiplies its weight. Adjacent through Not far fall as distance grows; Far and Very far rise. The dots are the distances and satisfactions the course case reports — a curve that misses them will not reproduce Layout 0's score.

A ramp runs between the distance where satisfaction is 1 and the distance where it is 0; bend curves it — 1 is a straight line, above 1 drops off faster at first, below 1 holds on longer. A bell peaks at its ideal distance and falls away on both sides, reaching half satisfaction one half-width out; it is the only form that can say “not too far, not too near”, where closer is not always better. The axis runs to 30 m — raise it for a larger facility.

Flow estimation matrix

Show empty trips Hide stations with no flow

Enter the loaded trips per period from each center to every other — these come from the process and organization design, or from flow tracking. A loaded trip runs from the source center's output station to the destination's input station. A figure you type turns green, because it is the only thing on this page that is not computed: the empty trips that return each vehicle to its next pickup, and every margin total, all follow from it. The diagonal takes no input, because a center never ships a loaded trip to itself.

Shaded italics are empty trips, running the other way round — from the source center's input station to the destination's output station, because a vehicle that has just unloaded is standing at the input dock. They are placed so that every center balances, arrivals equalling departures, at the least total empty travel. The diagonal carries the cheapest move of all: unload and reload within the same center.

The three layers are independent — turn on whichever combination you want to look at. Each route follows the same aisle path its distance was measured along, so nothing here crosses a block — a vehicle goes round, exactly as it would on the floor. Arrow width is proportional to trips per period. The heatmap adds the traffic up aisle by aisle instead, which is where congestion shows.

Distance matrix

Show every pair

How far each of those trips actually travels. Nothing here is typed in — every figure is measured on the layout above, so moving a center or sliding a station changes this table at once. A cell carries a figure only where the matrix above runs trips; tick Show every pair to measure the whole grid.

The two entries in a cell are measured between different points. A loaded trip leaves the source center's output station and ends at the destination's input station. An empty trip does the reverse — it starts where the vehicle just unloaded, the source's input station, and ends where it picks up next, the destination's output station. On the diagonal the vehicle crosses its own center from input dock to output dock, so that leg is measured straight through the block rather than around it.

Travel evaluation

Trips times distance, cell by cell — the metres a vehicle covers per period on each move. This is the number a layout is judged on: the two matrices above are its two halves, and everything a redesign is trying to cut shows up here. The margins split loaded from empty, because a metre driven empty earns nothing.

The average columns are metres per trip, not metres per period: total travel divided by the trips that made it. A center with a high average is one whose work arrives from far away, which is the case for moving it — high traffic at a short average is already well placed.
